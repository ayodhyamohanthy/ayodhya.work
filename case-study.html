01 — The problem

The bottleneck
was vocabulary.

Every question the business wanted answered went through a three-person data team. Analysts knew exactly what they needed; they just couldn't write the query, and the queue meant a two-day wait for a five-minute answer.

We'd tried a natural-language interface before. It failed because it was confidently wrong — it guessed, and people stopped trusting it. The real problem wasn't syntax. It was that nobody could see what the query was actually doing.

02 — Research

What we found.

01

Analysts think in outcomes, not joins

Every interview started with the business question, never the table. The interface had to begin where they began.

02

Trust came from visibility

People forgave a wrong result they could inspect. They never forgave a right result they couldn't.

03

Partial credit beats a blank page

A query that got someone 80% of the way was still a win — if they could see and edit the last 20%.

03 — The approach

Show the query,
always.

The core decision: the builder never hides the SQL. Every action in the visual editor writes to a live, editable query panel underneath. Drag a filter, watch the WHERE clause appear.

That single rule solved trust, taught SQL passively, and gave power users an escape hatch. It also meant we never had to build a second interface for the people who already knew what they were doing.

The constraint that shaped it

One backend engineer, and a query engine that couldn't be changed. Everything had to be expressible in the SQL dialect we already had — no new syntax, no server-side magic. The design had to live entirely in how we composed what was already possible.

05 — What I'd redo

Honest notes.

We shipped the saved-views feature two months late, and by then people had already built workarounds in spreadsheets. The habit formed before the feature arrived, and adoption never recovered.

Next time I'd ship the sharing and saving layer first, even in a rough state, and treat the builder as the thing that improves afterwards.
